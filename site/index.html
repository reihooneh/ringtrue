Make a bar that rings true.

A marimba bar is only a plank until its underside is carved. Ringtrue works out that arch so the bar's overtones land in tune, then listens to your real bar and says where to take wood off next.

Why the cut matters

Strike a plain, uncut bar and you hear a note plus overtones at 2.55 and 4.52 times its frequency (for a bar this thick; a thin one gives 2.76 and 5.40). Those don't belong to any chord, so the note sounds clangy and its pitch is hard to pin down.

Carving an arch underneath lowers the note and pulls the overtones into place. On a concert marimba the second partial sits exactly two octaves up (4 times the note) and the third at 10 times. That is what makes a marimba sound warm instead of like a tapped plank.

From board to note

  1. Design

    Pick the note, the wood and the bar's size. Ringtrue finds the length and the arch, and gives you a full-size template to print.

  2. Rough it out

    Cut the arch a little shallow. Wood can only come off, never go back on, so it's better to start sharp.

  3. Tap and trim

    Tap the bar near your phone. Ringtrue hears its partials, works out how your board differs from the book, and shows the next cuts.

What's inside

The maths runs in your browser: a C++ model of the bar as a beam that bends and shears, compiled to WebAssembly. It solves for the bar's partials, fits the cut with a least-squares optimiser, and finds the partials in a recording.
